# The Fighting Man

Adapted from a video essay on the Bygone Philosophy channel (YouTube id dLDt8VHyPl0). Revised for formal, written English; the argument and evidence are the original author's. The caption is a machine translation, and its errors were corrected against the sources it cites: "Mark" is Marcus Aurelius, "the quads on the Granois River" is Marcus's note "among the Quadi, on the Granua," "Delion" is Delium, and Socrates, born about 470 BC, was in his mid-forties at Delium rather than "almost 50." The closing paragraph that promotes the channel's app is kept, because it is part of the original and is the subject of a purpose question in the drill.

In the summer of 424 BC, the Athenian army broke and fled near the town of Delium. Men threw down their shields as the enemy cavalry rode through the fleeing ranks, cutting down anyone who looked afraid. One soldier did not run. He was in his mid-forties and barefoot, and he walked slowly away from the field, turning his head and calmly watching friend and enemy alike. An Athenian general who saw him that day said that anyone could tell, even from a distance, that this man would defend himself fiercely if he were attacked. So no one attacked him. The cavalry pursued the men who ran. The soldier was Socrates.

We remember Socrates as the man who asked questions in the marketplace. The men who stood beside him in battle remembered something else. At Potidaea, in a winter so cold that soldiers wrapped their feet in felt, he walked barefoot on the ice. He carried a wounded comrade off the battlefield and then declined the prize for bravery. The greatest philosopher of the Western tradition was also a soldier. This essay concerns that kind of man—not the brawler, and not the man who loses his temper and calls it strength, but something rarer: a man who knows how to fight, who has trained to fight, and who is calm for exactly that reason. He is formed in five ways.

First, he trains when no one is watching. In 1645, an aging swordsman sat in a cave in southern Japan and wrote a book. He had fought his first duel at thirteen, and by twenty-nine, he wrote, he had fought more than sixty and had never been defeated. His name was Miyamoto Musashi, and the book was The Book of Five Rings. One might expect such a man to write about talent, speed, or secret techniques. He wrote about repetition: a thousand days of practice to forge the spirit, and ten thousand days to polish it. Ten thousand days is more than twenty-seven years. Every martial tradition says the same thing in its own way. A Spartan boy entered the agoge at seven and trained until he was a man. A Roman legionary drilled with weapons twice as heavy as those he carried into battle, so that the battle itself would feel light. The fight lasts minutes. The fighter is made in the ten thousand days before it, when no one is watching and nothing appears to be happening.

Second, he conquers himself first. Musashi wrote one line that cuts deeper than the rest: “Today is victory over yourself of yesterday; tomorrow is your victory over lesser men.” The order matters: oneself first, then others. Centuries earlier and far to the west, a Roman emperor recorded the same principle in a private notebook. Marcus Aurelius spent most of his last decade on the frozen Danube frontier, at war with Germanic tribes. He closed the first book of the notebook that became the Meditations with a note that it was written among the Quadi, on the river Granua; the second book is headed “At Carnuntum,” a military camp. He was the most powerful man in the world, and he was at war. Yet what he wrote about at night was not the enemy across the river but his own anger, his reluctance to rise in the morning, and his vanity. At dawn he reminded himself that when he did not want to get up, he was rising to do the work of a human being. A man of this kind knows that his most dangerous opponent sleeps in his own bed.

Third, he welcomes a strong opponent. Most people pray for an easy life; the Stoics considered that a mistake. Epictetus, who was born a slave, told his students that difficulty is like a wrestling trainer who matches you with a rough young opponent. For what purpose? So that you may become an Olympic victor, and no one becomes a victor without sweat. His older contemporary Seneca went further. “I judge you unfortunate,” he wrote, “because you have never been unfortunate. You have passed through life without an antagonist; no one will know what you were capable of—not even you.” A person who has never been tested does not know who he is. He may be brave, or he may be a coward, and he will never find out. A man of this kind does not seek trouble, but when a strong opponent arrives—a hard year, a loss, someone determined to break him—he recognizes it for what it is: a sparring partner, and the means by which he will learn who he is.

Fourth, he wins without fighting when he can. This is what the bully never understands. One of the oldest surviving books on war, Sun Tzu's The Art of War, teaches that to fight and win a hundred battles is not the height of skill; the supreme skill is to subdue the enemy without fighting at all. Recall Socrates at Delium. He did not fight his way off the field. He walked off it, and no one dared to touch him. His calm did the fighting for him. This is the paradox at the heart of every martial tradition: the more dangerous a man becomes, the less he needs to prove it. The loud man, the threatening man, the man who starts arguments, is usually the frightened one. He fights because he is not sure that he could win. The true fighter is secure enough simply to walk away.

Fifth, he carries the struggle into ordinary life. None of these men reserved their discipline for the battlefield. Socrates showed the same steadiness at his trial, where he refused to beg for his life. Musashi held that his strategy applied to everything—to carpentry, to command, to the way a man enters a room. Marcus Aurelius wrote his notes on self-command not between battles but between the ordinary hours of reports, petitions, and fatigue. That is the real test. Most people can be brave for a moment; very few can be disciplined on an ordinary Tuesday. Your field is not Delium. It is the alarm you ignore, the temper you indulge, the difficult conversation you avoid, and the work you keep promising to begin. Each is a small battle, and in each you are either the person who trained for it or the person to whom it happens.

Each of these men kept a daily practice, and it can be adopted. In the morning, do what Marcus did. Before the day begins, tell yourself what it will bring: today I will meet people who are rude, ungrateful, arrogant, and dishonest. Then decide, before it happens, that none of them will make you like them. During the day, do what Epictetus taught. When something disturbs you, pause and say, “You are only an impression, and not at all what you appear to be.” Then ask a single question: is this within my power or not? If it is not, it is not your concern. From time to time, do something difficult on purpose. Seneca advised a friend to set aside a few days for the plainest food and the roughest clothing and then to ask, “Is this the condition I feared?” A man who has chosen hardship is not frightened when it arrives uninvited. In the evening, do what Seneca did before sleep: review the day. What weakness did I confront today? Where did I go wrong? What will I do better tomorrow? Morning, day, trial, and night—not once, but every day. Musashi's ten thousand days begin with the first.

Somewhere in every man's mind is a version of himself he has not yet become. That man wakes when he said he would. He keeps his word, even to himself. He stays calm when others lose their heads. He does the hardest thing first and finishes what he starts. Every man carries such an image, but few close the distance between who they are and who they were meant to be. The obstacle is not ignorance; anyone who reads Marcus Aurelius knows what to do. Knowing, however, is not the same as practicing.

Marcus had a camp, a notebook, and a war. Musashi had a sword and an opponent. Their training was built into every day of their lives; ours must be built deliberately. It begins by asking what stands between the user and that man, then draws on a tradition that answers the question—the Stoics, Musashi, the Zen masters, or Nietzsche—and assigns one of that tradition's practices each day, based on what the user did the day before, in less than half an hour. Day by day, the distance closes. The man you were meant to become is not someone else. He is you, trained.

Picture the field outside Delium: dust, screaming, men throwing down their shields, and one barefoot man walking slowly through it all, looking around. He was not the strongest man on that field. He was the most prepared. Years of campaigning and years of self-command had made him a man whom panic could not touch—a man who already knew what he would do. That is what a fighter is: not someone looking for a fight, but someone ready for one.
