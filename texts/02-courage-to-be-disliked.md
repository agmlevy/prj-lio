# The Courage to Be Disliked

Adapted from a video essay on the Bygone Philosophy channel (YouTube id hL-tPIuE2IQ). Revised for formal, written English; the argument and evidence are the original author's. The caption's "it's not a weakness, it's a flaw" contradicted itself and is rendered by its evident meaning: the reaction is built into the brain, not a personal failing.

Try to please everyone, and you will please no one. That is the moral of an ancient fable attributed to Aesop, and the whole story is worth retelling, because most people have lived some version of it.

A man and his son were walking to market, leading their donkey. A passerby laughed at them: how foolish, he said, to walk when they had a donkey to ride. So the man put his son on the donkey. Farther on, a group of men shook their heads at the lazy boy who rode while his father walked. The two switched places, and the father rode. Then two women passed and condemned the man for sitting in comfort while his small son stumbled along on foot. The father pulled the boy up beside him, and both rode. In the city, a crowd pointed and jeered: two riders on one small donkey would torture the poor animal. So the man and the boy dismounted, tied the donkey's legs to a pole, and carried it on their shoulders. The spectacle drew such laughter that people followed them all the way to the bridge. There the frightened donkey struggled free, the boy lost his grip on the pole, and the donkey fell into the river and drowned.

What makes the story uncomfortable is that everyone the travelers listened to had a reasonable point. No one gave them terrible advice. They simply tried to satisfy everyone, and they lost what they had set out with. Many people today carry a donkey of their own: a career chosen to please a parent, opinions softened to keep the peace, a whole life arranged around the comments of passersby.

Why do people keep doing this? The first thing to understand is that finding disapproval unbearable is not a personal weakness; it is built into the human brain. In 2003, researchers at the University of California, Los Angeles, placed volunteers in a brain scanner and asked them to play a simple computer game in which they tossed a virtual ball with two other players. The other players were not real, and for much of the game they stopped throwing the ball to the participant. The exclusion lasted only a few minutes, in a game that meant nothing. Yet the brain responded in the anterior cingulate cortex, a region associated with the distress of physical pain, and the more excluded participants said they felt, the more active that region became. The researchers concluded that social rejection appears to operate through a mechanism closely related to physical pain. The finding makes sense in light of human history. For most of that history, expulsion from the group was not an inconvenience; it could mean death. The brain therefore learned to treat disapproval as danger, and it has not updated. A raised eyebrow in a meeting, a comment beneath a post, a relative's disapproval of a decision—the body reacts to each as though the tribe were about to cast it out into the cold. The threat is not real, but it feels real, and many people will do almost anything to avoid that feeling, even to the point of betraying themselves.

The fear of disapproval does not stop at feeling; it can change what people are willing to call true. In 1951, the psychologist Solomon Asch showed participants a card bearing a single line, then a second card bearing three lines, and asked which of the three matched the first. The task was easy: people answering alone erred less than 1 percent of the time. In Asch's experiment, however, only one person in the room was a genuine subject. The others were confederates instructed to give the same wrong answer aloud, one after another, and the real participant answered near the end. On about a third of the trials, participants went along with an obviously incorrect answer, and three out of four conformed at least once. They looked at two lines that plainly did not match, heard the group agree, and said that the lines matched. The most important result came next. When Asch added a single confederate who gave the correct answer, conformity collapsed, falling from about a third of trials to about 5 percent. One person willing to stand apart allowed everyone else to tell the truth. That is what the courage to be disliked accomplishes: it frees not only the person who has it but also those who have been quietly waiting for someone else to move first.

How is such courage developed? One of the most useful answers comes from Alfred Adler, the Viennese psychiatrist who broke with Sigmund Freud in 1911 and founded his own school of psychology. Adler held that nearly all human problems are, at bottom, problems of relationships with other people. One idea from his work, popularized by the Japanese philosopher Ichiro Kishimi and the writer Fumitake Koga in their book The Courage to Be Disliked, is the separation of tasks. In any situation, one should ask: Whose task is this? The answer is found by asking who will ultimately live with the consequences of the choice. The career a person chooses is his task; whether his father approves of it is the father's. How a person treats others is his task; whether they like him for it is theirs. He can do his own part well—he can be honest, fair, and kind—but other people's reactions lie beyond his control, and they were never his burden. From this perspective, most difficulties in relationships arise from intruding on other people's tasks or allowing them to intrude on ours. Trying to control what others think of us is exactly such an intrusion: we take on a task that belongs to someone else and then fail at it every day. Being disliked, therefore, is not proof that something has gone wrong. Often it is evidence that a person is living by his own principles rather than someone else's. It is not a punishment. It is a receipt—the price of freedom.

The practice should begin on a small scale. This week, say one honest thing you would usually soften, and decline one request you would usually accept out of guilt. Notice the discomfort, and then observe what actually happens; usually it is far less than you feared. Each repetition teaches the brain that disapproval is not exile and that a person can survive being disliked. The idea, however, is easily distorted. The courage to be disliked is not permission to be unpleasant. Rudeness does not make anyone free; it only makes him rude. Adler placed what he called community feeling—a sense of belonging to others and contributing to their lives—at the center of a healthy life. The goal is not to stop caring about people. It is to stop needing their approval before doing right by them. A person who must be liked will lie to keep the peace. A person who has the courage to be disliked can afford to tell the truth and to be kind at the same time.

There is also a finding that almost no one expects. Psychologists studied what happens after strangers meet and talk for the first time, and then asked each person two questions: How much did you like the other person? How much do you think the other person liked you? Time after time, people underestimated how much they were liked. The researchers named this the liking gap. In one study of students sharing dormitory suites, the gap persisted for months. People, in other words, are probably liked more than they think—which means that many have paid a high price in honesty, in choices, and in direction to avoid a judgment that, for the most part, never came.

Return, finally, to the man and the boy on the road to market. At the outset they had everything they needed: a donkey, a destination, and two sound legs apiece. Every voice they obeyed made the journey worse, and in the end the very thing they were trying to protect was lost in the river. Some people will not approve of your choices. Let that be so; their approval is their task. Yours is to travel your own road at your own pace and to keep the donkey out of the water. Try to please everyone, and you will please no one. Satisfy your own conscience, and you will be free.
