# The Socially Intelligent

Adapted from a video essay on the Bygone Philosophy channel (YouTube id dZtQHre8Iy0), on Baltasar Gracián's The Art of Worldly Wisdom (1647). Revised for formal, written English; the argument and evidence are the original author's.

If you want someone to take your advice, do not let it sound like advice. Let it sound like something the person already knew and had simply forgotten. The idea is nearly four hundred years old. It comes from Baltasar Gracián, a Spanish Jesuit priest who in 1647 published a book of three hundred short maxims on dealing with people. Gracián observed that people will allow you to help them but will not allow you to outshine them. The best advice, he concluded, should arrive as a reminder of something forgotten rather than as directions to something the listener could not find. In practice, instead of saying, “You should do it this way,” one asks, “Didn't you mention a while ago that the other approach might work better?” The idea is the same; the reaction is entirely different. The first version makes the listener feel corrected. The second makes him feel sharp, and people support the ideas that make them feel sharp.

Gracián lived in a world of royal courts and church politics, where a single careless sentence could ruin a career. He published under his brother's name, and his own order eventually disciplined him for printing without permission. Nearly two centuries later, Schopenhauer considered the book worth translating into German himself. What follows is its core: the principles that genuinely change how people respond.

Social intelligence begins with careful listening, because almost no one says fully what he means. As Gracián put it, “The very truths which concern us most can only be half spoken.” A manager does not say, “I'm not sure you're ready”; she says, “Let's see how the next project goes.” A friend does not say, “I felt left out”; he says, “Looks like you all had a good night.” Most people reply to the half that was spoken. The socially intelligent person hears the half that was not.

Gracián adds a rule that seems odd at first. When you hear something good about yourself, be slow to believe it; when you hear something bad, be quicker. Flattery costs the flatterer nothing, whereas criticism costs the critic something—usually your goodwill. The person willing to risk annoying you is therefore often offering the more honest information.

Next comes what Gracián called finding each person's thumbscrew: the point of pressure that moves him. “All men are idolaters,” he wrote, “some of fame, others of self-interest, most of pleasure.” Everyone cares about one thing more than the rest. One colleague wants credit; another wants security and dislikes surprises; a third mainly wants to be liked. Present the same idea in the same way to all three, and two of them will be lost. Tell the first that the idea will be noticed, the second that it has been tested, and the third that the team is already enthusiastic. Gracián intended this as a means of steering people, and it can be used that way, but the more valuable lesson is simpler: much of the friction between people comes from giving others what we would want rather than what they actually want.

Nor should one decide too quickly who someone is. Gracián warns against being a slave to first impressions and notes that Alexander the Great always kept one ear for the other side. The first version of a story is merely the version of whoever arrived first; wait for the second telling, and sometimes the third. In judging people, words count, but actions count more. “You can tell metals by their ring,” he wrote, “and men by their voice.” One of the quickest tests available is to listen to how someone speaks about people who are not in the room, for that is how he will speak about you when you are absent.

That leads to conversation itself. Gracián believed that conversation is where a person's true character leaks out, and he quotes an ancient line: “Speak, that I may know thee.” Every time a person speaks, he gains a little ground or loses a little. Gracián did not believe in neutral speech.

The first way people lose ground is length. “Good things, when short, are twice as good.” Someone who takes four minutes to make a one-minute point does not seem thorough; he seems like someone to avoid in the hallway. State the heart of the matter and stop. If listeners want more, they will ask, and a person who is asking is in a very different mood from a person who is enduring.

The second is talking about oneself. Gracián's reasoning is precise: a person who talks about himself either praises himself, which is vain, or disparages himself, which looks small. Either way, the listener grows uncomfortable. The humble brag and the endless self-deprecating joke are the same mistake approached from opposite sides.

The third is complaining, and Gracián's rule on it is among the cleverest in the book. When you complain about how someone treated you, he observes, you do not earn sympathy; you earn a reputation as someone who can be treated that way. You have shown others where the door is. His alternative is to speak of the good things people have done for you. Mention in front of others how generous someone was, and you quietly set the standard for how you expect to be treated. Complaint lowers the bar; praise raises it.

Closely related is the advice not to reveal a sore spot. Gracián's image is an injured finger: once everyone knows it is there, everything seems to knock against it. If people learn that a certain remark bothers you, some will repeat it simply to watch the effect. An advertised insecurity is a handle that someone else can grab.

Nor should one defend oneself against accusations no one has made. “To excuse oneself before there is occasion is to accuse oneself.” Walk into a meeting explaining why a report is late when no one had noticed, and now everyone has noticed. Answer the question that was asked, not the one you fear.

Then there is praise. Gracián describes a familiar type: the person who flatters you by disparaging whoever is absent. It feels like intimacy. Only shallow people fall for it, he says, because anyone paying attention realizes that such a person speaks badly of everyone to everyone. Do the opposite. Carry good opinions from room to room. Speak well of people behind their backs, because such words tend to reach them, and you will become someone whose word is trusted precisely because it is never poison.

On disagreement, Gracián is realistic rather than noble. When you dispute someone's view, he notes, the person takes it as an insult, because you are condemning his judgment. That does not mean agreeing with everything; it means choosing one's ground. Some people turn every pleasant evening into a debate, finding the flaw in every film, every restaurant, and every plan. That habit, Gracián says, may prove a person clever, but it marks him as a fool. “Grit grates most in delicacies,” he adds; no one wants sand in dessert. Save disagreement for matters that count.

Humor follows the same rule. Take teasing well, Gracián advises, but be careful in handing it out, for “the most serious matters have arisen out of jest.” Before joking about someone, know how much he can bear. When a joke is aimed at you, laughing along reads as strength, while showing that you are stung only makes the moment worse for everyone.

Above all, do not be made of glass. Gracián describes people so easily wounded that they find insult everywhere, forcing everyone around them to tiptoe. He calls them, for the most part, egoists ruled by their moods. Fragility does make others handle a person carefully at first. Eventually, however, it makes them handle him less, because being near him has become work.

Two skills that almost no one practices deliberately are asking and refusing. In asking, timing does most of the work. Gracián advises approaching people when they are in good spirits—after a good meal, or just after something has gone their way. “The days of joy are the days of favor.” Never ask immediately after someone else has been refused, because the person has just grown comfortable saying no, and never immediately after bad news. The same request made at a different hour can receive a different answer. This is not trickery; it is simply attention to the person in front of you.

On refusing, Gracián offers one of the finest lines in the book: “A gilded no is more satisfactory than a dry yes.” Some people agree so grudgingly that they receive no credit for agreeing; others decline so graciously that the other person leaves feeling respected. A refusal should not be blunt and final. Let it land gently. Give a genuine reason and, where honesty permits, leave some hope. “Not this month, but ask me again in the spring” is often received better than a resentful yes.

None of this survives a bad temper, and on this point Gracián concedes nothing. “Never act in a passion,” he warns; if you do, all is lost. His reasoning is that a person cannot act in his own interest when he is not himself. Spectators, he observes, often read a game better than the players, simply because they are calm. When you feel your temper rising, step back. Leave the room. Leave the message in drafts until morning. A few seconds of anger, in his words, can cost many days of repentance.

Patience with difficult people belongs here as well. Gracián cites the Stoic Epictetus, who regarded forbearance as half of wisdom. He then adds an uncomfortable observation: we usually must bear the most from precisely the people on whom we depend. He calls this a useful lesson in self-control, and he is right.

When conflict does arise, one line is worth remembering: “Few can do us good; almost anyone can do us harm.” No one knows whom another person will become or whom he will come to know. Relationships, therefore, should not end in explosions. If distance from someone is necessary, let it look like drifting rather than burning. A friendship that fades costs almost nothing; one that ends in a confrontation gives the other person a story to tell about you for years.

If someone does dislike you, make use of him. “A wise man gets more use from his enemies than a fool from his friends.” Critics hunt for your flaws, which means they conduct an inspection you would never think to pay for. Friends, being kind, look away. Gracián goes further, arguing that flattery is more dangerous than hatred, because flattery conceals the stains that hatred would force you to clean. Take whatever is true in the criticism, correct it, and quietly leave your critics nothing to say.

Part of social intelligence lies in what one withholds. Gracián's rule here is to leave while still hungry. The best conversation of the evening is the one left while it was still good, and the friend seen a little less often is the one people are glad to see. The person who pushes in where he is not wanted, Gracián warns, is pushed out the same way.

The same principle applies to knowledge. Do not reveal everything in the first conversation. People who allow themselves to be sounded to the bottom, Gracián says, lose respect, because guesses about a person's depth impress more than a full inventory of it. Depth revealed gradually holds attention far longer than depth surrendered all at once.

Truth belongs in this discussion, too, and Gracián treats it with care. A single lie, he says, can destroy an entire reputation for honesty, so one must not lie. Yet he also calls truth the lancet of the heart—a surgeon's blade. Not every true thing needs to be said, and not every true thing needs to be said now. Deciding to be honest is easy. The real skill lies in choosing which honest thing will actually help, and when.

Here is the part that most people who quote the book omit: some of it is cold. Gracián advises keeping people dependent on you, because “hope has a good memory, gratitude a bad one.” He discusses finding people's weak points in order to move their will. A reader who saw only those pages would take the book for a manual for manipulators. But consider how it begins and how it ends. The second maxim holds that character and intellect are the two poles of a person, and that either one without the other achieves only half of what is possible. The three hundredth maxim, the last after all the strategy and caution, reads: “In one word, be a saint.” Virtue, Gracián writes, is what makes a person trustworthy and respected—loved in life and remembered after death. Everything else, he concludes, is a jest.

That principle underlies all three hundred maxims. Reading people, choosing words, controlling one's temper, and knowing when to leave are tools. In decent hands, they make people feel understood and safe. In the wrong hands, they are eventually detected, however skilled the performance. Gracián devoted an entire book to teaching readers how to handle people, and then made his final rule the one that mattered most: be good first, and then be clever enough that it shows.
