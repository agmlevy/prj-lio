# How to Speak Articulately

Adapted from a video essay on the Bygone Philosophy channel (YouTube id G5ORE_8TTBo). Revised for formal, written English; the argument and evidence are the original author's.

“The limits of my language mean the limits of my world.” Ludwig Wittgenstein wrote that sentence a century ago, and it may be one of the most quietly devastating in philosophy. If it is true, then every word a person cannot find is a thought he cannot fully have, and every idea he cannot express is, as far as the world is concerned, an idea he never had. The world is listening. Within moments of hearing someone speak, people judge how intelligent he is, how confident he is, and whether he is worth following. Fair or not, the voice is the window through which others judge the mind.

Most people misunderstand what it means to speak well. Being articulate is not a matter of large words, rapid delivery, an absence of pauses, or an impressive sound. It is something simpler and considerably harder: it is clear thinking made audible.

A line attributed to Cato the Elder, the Roman statesman, contains the whole secret: “Grasp the subject, and the words will follow.” Most people who stumble when they speak do not have a language problem; they have a thinking problem. They begin a sentence before they know where it is going and then search for its destination in real time, in front of everyone. That search is what listeners hear as “um,” “uh,” “like,” and “you know.” The first task, therefore, is completed before the speaker opens his mouth: he must know his point—not his topic, but his point. “My job” is a topic. “I am leaving my job because I have stopped growing” is a point. The test is simple: a speaker who cannot state his point in one sentence is not ready to state it in ten. Once he has that sentence, he should give it a spine: state the point, give the reason, offer an example, and return to the point. The pattern sounds almost too simple, yet it is how clear thinkers have always spoken, and it ensures that the listener always knows where he is and where he is being taken.

Aristotle taught that the first virtue of good speech is clarity, and his reasoning was blunt. Language exists to carry meaning from one mind to another, so speech that fails to make its meaning plain has failed at the one thing it exists to do. This is where many speakers undermine themselves by trying to sound intelligent. They reach for the longest word, the most tangled sentence, the vague phrase that sounds profound. Complexity, however, is usually a hiding place; it conceals the fact that the speaker is unsure of what he means. Articulate people do the opposite. They use ordinary words with extraordinary precision: short sentences, concrete examples, one idea at a time. When an idea is abstract, they make it physical. Rather than saying that a team lacks coordination, they say that it is five men rowing in different directions. The mind forgets abstractions, but it remembers pictures. When a truly intelligent person explains something, listeners do not walk away impressed by the vocabulary; they walk away understanding.

Blaise Pascal once closed a letter by apologizing for its length: he had made it longer than usual, he explained, only because he had not had time to make it shorter. Saying less is harder than saying more, because it forces the speaker to decide what matters. Most speech is padded with words that carry nothing—“basically,” “honestly,” “kind of,” “I just feel like.” Each is small; together they bury the meaning and drain the speaker's authority. A speaker should learn to hear them and then cut them. He should also attend closely to how he finishes. Weak speakers trail off: “And so, yeah, I don't know.” Strong speakers land the sentence and stop.

Then there are hedges: “I think maybe,” “I'm not sure, but,” “This could be wrong, but.” Sometimes uncertainty is honest, and then it should be stated once, plainly. Most hedges, however, are not honesty but armor. They protect the speaker from being wrong by ensuring that he never fully says anything. A speaker who believes something should say it: “This is the right move,” not “I don't know, maybe it could possibly be.” Conviction is not arrogance; it is simply meaning what one says.

That leads to the most underrated tool in speaking: silence. Most filler words arise from a single fear, the fear of a gap. Speakers believe that a pause makes them look lost, so they fill it with noise, but the opposite is true. A pause makes a speaker look certain. It tells the room that he is choosing his words rather than grabbing at them, and it gives listeners a moment to absorb what was just said. Whenever an “um” is coming, the speaker should close his mouth and let the silence stand. To him it will feel endless; to everyone else it will sound like composure. He should also slow down. Nervous speakers rush because some part of them wants to escape. The right pace is that of someone who expects to be listened to. Statements should end on a falling note, not a rising one, because a sentence that lifts at the end sounds like a question—as if the speaker were asking permission to believe himself.

Return now to Wittgenstein. If language is the boundary of one's world, then building a vocabulary is not a matter of display but of perception. A man who has only the word “bad” cannot distinguish among disappointed, betrayed, exhausted, and ashamed. If he cannot name the difference, he cannot explain it, and often he cannot recognize it in himself. Precise words make a person clearer not only to others but also to himself. Such a vocabulary is not built by memorizing lists. It is built by reading—real writing by people who used language well—and by reading it aloud, so that the mouth learns the shape of a good sentence. A word worth keeping should be used three times in the week it is found, until it belongs to the speaker.

Articulate speech, moreover, concerns more than the speaker. Zeno of Citium, the founder of Stoicism, is said to have remarked that we have two ears and one mouth so that we may listen more and talk less. The most articulate people in any room are almost always its best listeners. They hear the whole question before answering it, and they answer what was asked rather than what they wished to discuss. Because they listened, their words land exactly where they are needed. Most people, by contrast, listen while preparing a reply, and it shows: their answers miss by a few inches. Those who listen fully find that their words begin to hit the target.

Beneath every technique lies one final layer. Seneca repeated an old Greek saying: “As a man lives, so he speaks.” Disordered speech usually comes from a disordered mind, and vague words often come from a vaguely lived life—from avoiding hard thoughts and never deciding what one actually believes. No one can speak clearly about what he refuses to think clearly about. The most powerful speaking practice, then, is not a speaking practice at all. It is thinking: writing things down and deciding what one believes and why. When someone finally asks, the answer is already there, waiting.

The practice can begin today. Read aloud for ten minutes a day from something well written. Once a day, choose one idea and explain it into your phone for sixty seconds, using the pattern of point, reason, example, point. Listen to the recording, count the filler words, and repeat the explanation with pauses where the fillers were. Before every important conversation, write your point in a single sentence. When someone asks you a question, take one breath before answering. Collect words you love, and use them until they belong to you. Cut every word that is not doing work. After thirty days of this practice, the people around you will notice. They will not be able to say exactly what changed; they will simply find themselves listening. The limits of one's language really are the limits of one's world. Every word mastered pushes that boundary a little further out, and every clear sentence is a piece of the mind made visible. Speak, then, like someone who has thought it through—because from now on, you will have.
