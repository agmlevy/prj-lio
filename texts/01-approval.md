# What Happens When You Stop Seeking Approval

Adapted from a video essay on the Bygone Philosophy channel (YouTube id Yw6Ip-enx1Y). Revised for formal, written English; the argument and evidence are the original author's.

“Every man's chief and real existence,” Arthur Schopenhauer wrote in the 1850s, “is in his own skin, and not in other people's opinions.” In the same essay he warned of what becomes of the man who forgets this: he becomes the slave of what other people are pleased to think. The word is exact. Such a man is not a servant but a slave, for a servant can quit, while a man who needs approval carries his master everywhere: into every meeting, every conversation, and every decision about what to say, what to wear, and what to want. He laughs a little louder at the joke. He agrees a little faster than he believes. He explains himself to people who never asked. At night he replays the day, checking how he came across. Most men live this way without noticing it, and most never learn what happens when it stops.

What follows describes, in the order in which it occurs, what happens when a man stops seeking approval.

The first change is awareness: he notices how often he was doing it. The moment he resolves to stop, he begins to catch himself—rewriting a message four times so that it sounds right, softening an opinion before anyone has pushed back, telling a story about himself that is slightly better than the truth, explaining a refusal when “no” was a complete answer. The discovery is uncomfortable, because it reveals how much of his day was a performance and how much of his energy went into managing other people's impressions instead of living his own life. He should not resist the discomfort. It is the first sign that he is waking up.

The second change is that some people react. When he stops agreeing with everything, someone will be surprised. When he stops saying yes to every request, someone will be disappointed. When he stops laughing at jokes that are not funny, someone will notice the silence, and a few people may withdraw. Those people have not lost him. They have lost a version of him that was built to please them. A relationship that survives only as long as he performs was never a relationship with him; it was a relationship with the performance. This is the stage at which most men turn back, because the discomfort feels like proof that they have made a mistake. It is not proof of a mistake. It is the cost of becoming visible.

The third change is that his energy returns. The psychologist Jennifer Crocker spent years studying what she called contingencies of self-worth: the domains in which people believe they must succeed in order to feel worthy. In one study at the University of Michigan, her team followed more than 600 college freshmen through their first year. More than a third reported that other people's approval was important to how they valued themselves. Students whose self-worth rested on external sources—approval, appearance, and outperforming others—fared worse. They reported more stress and more anger, drank and used drugs more, and showed more symptoms of disordered eating. These links held even after the researchers accounted for how high or low the students' overall self-esteem was. The implication is significant. It was not low confidence that harmed these students; it was where their confidence was stored. When a man's worth is held in other people's hands, every interaction becomes a test he can fail. He is always on trial, and a man who is always on trial is always exhausted. When he stops seeking approval, the trial ends, and the energy it consumed returns to him.

The fourth change concerns his work. In 1973, at a nursery school on the Stanford campus, the psychologists Mark Lepper, David Greene, and Richard Nisbett studied children who already enjoyed drawing. They told some of the children that if they drew, they would receive a Good Player Award, a certificate with a gold seal and a red ribbon. The children drew and received their awards. One to two weeks later, the researchers quietly set the markers out again during free play and observed. The children who had drawn for the award now spent about half as much of their free time drawing as children who had never been promised anything. They had not lost the ability to draw; they had stopped drawing for themselves. An activity pursued out of love had become an activity pursued for a ribbon, and when the ribbon disappeared, so did the desire. Approval-seeking does the same thing to a man's work. He stops asking whether the work is good and starts asking whether it will be liked. He writes what earns applause, trains for how it looks, and chooses the career that sounds impressive. Gradually, the things he once loved begin to feel like obligations. When he stops seeking approval, he recovers the boy who drew because he wanted to. The work becomes his own again, and work that is truly one's own is almost always better, because attention has returned from the audience to the work.

The fifth change is that his relationships deepen. Crocker and her colleague Amy Canevello later followed college students who were sharing dormitory rooms with strangers. Some entered these relationships focused on protecting their image—on making sure they looked good. Others entered them genuinely trying to support the other person. The image-focused students were less likely to report that their relationships were improving, while those who offered support out of real concern built the closest and most supportive relationships. As Canevello put it, we get support by being supportive. Here lies the hidden irony. The man who seeks approval believes he is focused on other people, but he is focused on himself and on how he is coming across. He is not listening to the person in front of him; he is watching his own reflection in that person's eyes. Once he no longer needs approval, he can finally attend to others. He can listen without planning his next impressive line, and he can tell a friend a hard truth because he no longer fears losing the friend's good opinion. People feel the difference.

The final change is a paradox: when a man stops seeking approval, people begin to respect him more. Psychologists who study flattery, beginning with Edward Jones in the 1960s, describe what is known as the ingratiator's dilemma. The more obviously a person depends on someone's approval, the more that someone suspects his praise and agreement are a strategy. Flattery is therefore most likely to fail precisely when it is most needed. People sense the need behind it, and need, displayed openly, makes everything a man says worth less. The man who does not need approval is different. When he agrees, his agreement means something, because he would have said so if he disagreed. When he offers praise, it is believed. His yes is worth something because his no is real.

This claim requires a qualification. To stop seeking approval is not to stop caring what anyone thinks. A man who ignores every voice is not free; he is merely deaf. Approval and feedback are different things. Approval concerns whether people like him; feedback concerns whether he is right. A strong man stops pursuing the first and actively seeks the second, from the few people whose judgment he has good reason to trust.

The process begins with observation. For one week, keep a count of every occasion on which you explain yourself to someone who did not ask, apologize for something that was not wrong, or agree with something you do not believe. Do not judge these moments; simply count them, because a habit cannot be broken until it is seen. Then refuse one request without offering an excuse. Notice how uncomfortable the refusal feels, and notice that nothing collapses. State one honest opinion where you would ordinarily remain silent—not to provoke anyone, but to allow your actual view to exist aloud. Do one thing this week purely because you want to, and tell no one about it: no post, no story, no audience. Finally, choose deliberately the small number of people whose judgment you will take seriously. Everyone else's opinion is weather. You notice it, and you keep walking.

Schopenhauer was right. A man's real existence is in his own skin—not in the mind of the person he is trying to impress, not in the comments, and not in the reaction of the room. That is what truly happens when he stops seeking approval: he comes home, and for the first time in a long while, the man living his life is himself.
